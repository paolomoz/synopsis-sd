“Force fields” have long captured our imagination — the invisible shields of science-fiction lore that protect starships and superheroes from harm. But in the world of scientific discovery, force fields play a much different role: They are mathematical models that let us peer into the atomic heart of matter itself.

Now, thanks to breakthroughs in artificial intelligence, GPU acceleration, and advanced simulation platforms, force fields are poised to make a transformative leap — from science fiction to mainstream R&D.

In fact, we predict that in 2026 machine-learned force fields (MLFFs), which are also known as machine-learned potentials (MLPs), will enable atomistic simulations that are 10,000 times faster than today’s quantum-theoretical methods like Density Functional Theory (DFT).

What’s more, this speedup will usher in a new era of discovery and innovation.

What are force fields?

For decades, atomistic simulations have powered major advances in materials science, electronics, and pharmaceuticals.

Central to these computer-based simulations is the accurate modeling of atomic interactions. To achieve this, scientists often use force fields — mathematical frameworks that define how atoms attract, repel, bind together (forming molecules or crystals), and evolve (over time and under influence). Force fields provide the “rules of engagement” for computer models by describing the forces at each point in space or the potential energy landscapes, such as electrostatic potentials surrounding charged particles, that guide molecular dynamics.

Atomistic simulations enable researchers to investigate phenomena such as chemical reactions during catalysis, protein folding pathways, and the response of materials to heat and pressure. The ability to do so virtually — before investing in costly, time-consuming physical experiments — is critical when developing and optimizing innovative materials, new drugs, and advanced nano-devices.

Up until now, however, these fields of research have been hamstrung by a fundamental bottleneck: the trade-off between accuracy and computational feasibility.

Traditional force fields, which use fixed formulas and empirical parameters to model atomic behavior, are computationally efficient and effective for simple systems. But they struggle with complex materials and chemical reactions, lacking the flexibility needed for cutting-edge discovery and innovation.

Conversely, simulations based on DFT — which model atomic interactions at the quantum level — offer far better precision but come with a steep computational cost. Simulating a few thousand atoms using traditional DFT methods can take days or weeks, and large-scale, realistic modeling involving hundreds of thousands or even millions of atoms has remained wholly impractical.

Fortunately, new types of force field models have emerged in recent years that don’t demand such trade-offs.

The emergence of machine-learned force fields (MLFFs)

Machine-learned force fields (MLFFs) represent a transformative leap in atomistic simulation and scientific research.

Instead of relying on rigid, predefined formulas, MLFFs leverage artificial intelligence (typically deep neural networks) trained on quantum-mechanical data from DFT calculations. This training allows them to learn the underlying patterns of interatomic interactions and capture the complex energy landscapes that govern molecular behavior. In familiar chemical environments, these models can deliver near-DFT accuracy while running thousands of times faster than traditional quantum simulations.

Unlike conventional force fields, MLFFs can adapt to new chemical spaces and be retrained as additional data becomes available, making them far more versatile for evolving research needs.

The result isn’t just faster simulations; MLFFs make atomistic modeling practical for a much wider range of problems, giving researchers the ability to explore more realistic scenarios and generate insights that were previously out of reach. Using MLFFs, researchers can:

  • Simulate much larger systems, scaling from thousands to millions of atoms.
  • Explore longer time scales, from picoseconds to nanoseconds and beyond.
  • Model complex phenomena like thermal transport, mechanical failure, diffusion, and chemical reactions.

All of these capabilities are critical for the research and development of next-generation materials and devices.

The technological engine behind MLFFs

The emergence and anticipated impact of MLFFs can be traced to recent hardware and software innovations. And our prediction of a 10,000× speedup in simulation workflows will be driven by four technological pillars:

  1. Artificial intelligence. MLFFs learn atomic interactions from vast pre-existing datasets of quantum calculations, and they can be rapidly adapted and fine-tuned for new chemical environments using synthetic training data that is generated in real time.
  2. GPU acceleration. Most MLFFs are natively designed to run on GPUs. Advances in algorithms, libraries, and hardware will continue to provide improvements in both training and simulation speeds.
  3. Network-based scaling. Because GPU memory remains a limiting factor for MLFFs, high-speed interconnects that allow many GPUs to work together seamlessly will be critical — not just for boosting performance, but for scaling simulations to handle much larger system sizes.
  4. Advanced simulation platforms. Platforms like Synopsys QuantumATK bring together MLFFs running on multi-GPU architectures with advanced atomistic simulation algorithms. These platforms integrate multiple computational approaches — such as DFT — within a single, flexible Python interface, making it easy for users to customize workflows, including active learning and fine-tuning of MLFFs.

The availability of these technologies and resulting democratization of advanced simulation will be a boon for research and innovation. Tasks that once required supercomputers or years of manual effort can now be completed in hours using GPU-accelerated systems. And scientists across industries can access these powerful methods through professional-grade software platforms designed for ease of use and scalability.

As more startups, academic labs, and enterprises take advantage of these capabilities, we believe atomistic simulations will become as routine in R&D as computer-aided design (CAD) became in mechanical engineering decades ago. MLFFs play a central role in this shift, enabling large-scale, high-accuracy simulations of chemical reactions, thermal transport, and other complex phenomena that were once impractical to study at this level.

The anticipated impact: from semiconductors to pharmaceuticals

AI-powered, GPU-accelerated force fields are already making an impact across multiple sectors. And with a 10,000× speedup in simulation workflows compared to quantum methods like DFT, we expect to see a number of breakthroughs in 2026:

  • Development of advanced chip architectures. MLFFs are enabling engineers to simulate complex interfaces and low-concentration impurities, helping accelerate the development of next-generation chips and qualify the impact of defects before fabrication.
  • Discovery of new battery materials. Scientists are using MLFFs to model solid-electrolyte interfaces and ion transport with far greater speed and accuracy, supporting the discovery of new chemistries for electric vehicles and grid storage.
  • Precision drug design. Drug developers are simulating protein-ligand interactions with atomic-level detail, enabling rapid, accurate screening of drug candidates and dramatically reducing time-to-discovery.

The future of simulation has arrived

AI-powered, GPU-accelerated force fields hold tremendous promise for research and innovation. When combined with advanced simulation platforms, they enable highly accurate modeling of atomic interactions at a fraction of the computational cost of traditional DFT methods.

2026 will be a transformative year in this area — one where force fields redefine the boundaries of atomistic simulation, making previously unthinkable modeling and discoveries routine. With workflows that are 10,000× faster, researchers and engineers will unlock new frontiers in materials science, electronics, energy, and medicine.

The age of high-fidelity simulation for mainstream R&D is here. And its impact will be felt across industries and around the world.
